\begin{abstract}
A UMI-style device carries a wrist camera for the policy and a pose tracker for the labels: SLAM on that camera, an IMU, a VR controller or a fixed external camera. We replace both with one egocentric camera worn on the collector's head. It sees the fiducial ball and the jaw tags on an otherwise electronics-free glove and the printed tiles taped around the workspace, so every frame carries the gripper's pose in a tile-fixed world frame and the jaw width, with no cable, no IMU and no SLAM. At deployment the same camera rides a cheap servo-driven printed arm beside the robot and keeps the gripper in view the way the head kept the hand in view, driven by a linear viewpoint model fitted on one glove session. The labels are checked against the robot's encoders: \SI{6.5}{mm} median tool-point error, \SI{1.6}{\degree} rotation, \SI{93}{}--\SI{100}{\percent} of frames labelled per session against \SI{78}{}--\SI{92}{\percent} for a fixed external camera that the collector's body hides, and a policy trained on head-camera labels instead of encoders pays \SI{3}{\percent} in validation loss. One person records 2.0--3.1 demonstrations per minute against 1.6 by teleoperation. Adding 194 glove segments to 172 teleoperated episodes lowers ACT's loss on held-out teleoperated episodes from 0.318 to 0.263; added to 48 teleoperated episodes it reaches 0.295, the level of 96--172 teleoperated episodes alone, and on the robot the 48-episode co-trained policy succeeds in \grid{X/25} trials against \grid{X/25} for 48 teleoperated episodes alone and \grid{X/25} for the full 172. Against the conventional wrist-camera recipe and a fixed-camera recipe trained from the same demonstrations the head-camera policy \grid{succeeds in X/25 vs X/25 and X/25}. The viewpoint model is naive and the arm is a \$\todo{N} print, so these numbers are a floor for what one moving camera can do. \todo{final numbers from the grid}
\end{abstract}

\section{The idea}
\label{sec:idea}
Every UMI-style device answers two questions: what does the policy see, and where was the gripper. The standard answers are a wrist camera and a pose tracker on the same wrist, SLAM over the camera \citep{chi2024umi}, an IMU, a VR controller \citep{vrtracking2024} or, in our earlier glove \citep{yamumi2026}, a fixed 4K camera over the workspace, which gives absolute labels but ties collection to a tripod and loses the hand behind the collector's own body. Two of the open problems this workshop names are in-the-wild tracking that does not rest on SLAM, and the viewpoint gap between what a handheld device records and what the robot sees. Our answer to both is one camera, worn. The collector's head follows the hand by habit, keeps the gripper roughly centred at arm's length and is never occluded by the torso; from its view the fiducial ball gives the pose, the jaw tags give the width, and printed AprilTag tiles taped on the table give the world frame, so the glove itself carries no electronics at all. The same camera is the policy's observation. On the robot a small servo-driven printed arm carries it and imitates the head from the robot's joint state alone, identically during teleoperation and rollouts, so training and deployment share one viewpoint distribution. The price is a moving camera at deployment; the claim is that this price buys the wrist camera, the tracker and the cable, and that the policy is as good. A fixed camera was kept only as an ablation.

\section{System}
\label{sec:system}

\paragraph{Collection.}
The glove is the fiducial-tracked YAM gripper replica of \citet{yamumi2026}: the robot's own fingers on a rack-and-pinion linear mechanism, an 11-face \SI{15}{mm} ArUco dodecahedron on the back of the hand, two AprilTags in the jaws. The collector wears an Arducam B0587 4K camera \todo{mount: how it is worn, weight, focus setting} at \SI{15}{Hz}, exposure locked short enough to freeze hand motion. Four \SI{60}{mm} AprilTag tiles are taped around the workspace (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}); their relative layout is measured once from the head-camera footage itself (\S\ref{sec:labels}). Cameras roll continuously and a key press marks episode boundaries; the collector records 2.0--3.1 pick-and-place demonstrations per minute (median episode \SI{6}{}--\SI{8}{s}), against 1.6 per minute by teleoperation of the same task on the same arm (median \SI{14}{s}).

\paragraph{Labels from the head camera.}
\label{sec:labels}
Each frame yields three things from one image. (i) The camera's pose in the tile frame, from every tile in view; (ii) the ball's pose in the camera, by fused PnP over the visible faces; (iii) the jaw width, from the jaw tags. (i) and (ii) compose to the gripper's pose in the tile frame, and a tile-to-robot-base link, fitted once from a session in which the robot carried the ball, maps it into the robot's base frame for the same joint-space IK the teleoperated labels use. Two things make (i) robust on a moving camera. First, the tile layout is refit from the footage: the tile positions declared from the first fixed-camera session were inconsistent at the few-millimetre level, so solves over three or four tiles ran at \SI{3.4}{px} RMS while any pair ran under \SI{1}{px}; refitting the layout from single-tile poses observed in one session brings the four-tile solve to \SI{0.5}{px} and the disagreement between two single-tile camera poses on the same frame from \SI{10}{}--\SI{13}{mm} to \SI{4}{}--\SI{9}{mm} median. Second, frames that show only one tile are chained to the last accepted pose under a motion bound (\SI{0.6}{m/s}, \SI{120}{\degree/s}) instead of being discarded. What remains blind is cut: stretches longer than \SI{0.3}{s} without a label split an episode into segments, and segments under \SI{1}{s} are dropped, so a blind moment costs its frames and not the episode. Per session, \SI{0.7}{}--\SI{7.4}{\percent} of frames are cut this way; the same demonstrations labelled from the fixed external camera lose \SI{8}{}--\SI{22}{\percent}, because the collector's body hides the ball, and fragment into 259 segments where the head camera keeps 200.

\paragraph{The robot's head.}
A six-joint SO-101 arm beside the YAM carries the same camera (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}c). At the start of a session it parks at the median head pose of the glove sessions, closed-loop against the tiles, and calibrates its own view axes against them. During teleoperation and during rollouts it reads only the YAM's joint state: forward kinematics gives the gripper's position, a fixed gain keeps the gripper's bearing in the camera where it was at park, and a residual model fitted on the glove sessions reproduces how the head's viewpoint depends on the hand's position. Commands pass through a low-pass filter, a rate cap of \SI{50}{\degree/s} and a workspace box, and the resulting camera motion lags the gripper by about \SI{0.4}{s}, with a median camera position error of \SI{16}{mm} (\SI{34}{mm} p90) against the tiles. The gripper's framing in the robot's head-camera footage (centroid at 0.37/0.62 of the image width/height) matches the collector's (0.38/0.60). The kinematic model of the arm is fitted once from 60 tile-verified poses (\SI{7}{mm}/\SI{0.7}{\degree} residual) and the camera is released to the recorder after calibration; the positioner is otherwise invisible to the policy.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
Teleoperation under the positioner gave 178 episodes (38.6k frames at \SI{15}{Hz}); 6 are held out as the validation set for every arm. Four glove sessions gave 198 demonstrations, 200 segments after blind cutting (50.0k frames), 6 held out. Every policy is ACT \citep{zhao2023act} trained with LeRobot \citep{lerobot2024}: one recipe, 50k steps, joint-space actions, best checkpoint by validation loss on the robot's held-out episodes. The glove timeline is resampled to teleoperation pace and the measured width is mapped to the robot's commanded value, as in \citet{yamumi2026}.

\paragraph{Label accuracy.}
With the ball on the YAM and the encoders as reference, the head-camera chain places the tool point with \SI{6.5}{mm} median / \SI{14.1}{mm} p90 error and \SI{1.55}{\degree} / \SI{2.70}{\degree} rotation (5\,065 frames, \SI{91}{\percent} inliers), after the one-time base link absorbs the constant offset. Downstream, a policy trained on head-camera labels instead of encoders (arm 6 against arm 1), scored on the same encoder-labelled held-out episodes, reaches 0.328 against 0.318, a \SI{3}{\percent} price for the labels, and succeeds in \grid{X/25} rollouts against \grid{X/25}.

\paragraph{Co-training and the teleoperation budget.}
Adding the glove set to all 172 teleoperated episodes lowers the robot-validation loss from 0.318 to 0.263 (\SI{17}{\percent}); added to 48 teleoperated episodes it lowers it from 0.464 to 0.295. Fig.~\ref{fig:budget} shows the full curve: the glove set is worth 65--100 teleoperated episodes by log-linear interpolation of the teleop-only curve, i.e. 2--3 glove demonstrations per teleoperated one, and the 48-episode mix lands between 96 and 172 teleoperated episodes alone. The gain shrinks with budget (0.075 at 24 episodes down to 0.011 at 96, measured against the full mix), so glove data stretches a small teleoperated set but does not replace the last few dozen episodes. \section{Limitations and what is next}
\label{sec:discussion}
The tile layout is refit from one session by median single-tile poses rather than bundle-adjusted; a proper adjustment should bring the single-tile jitter below the current \SI{4}{}--\SI{9}{mm}. The positioner lags the gripper by \SI{0.4}{s} and hits joint limits on the largest sweeps; a lighter camera or a faster arm would close that. The head camera also sees the collector's arm and the glove's fingers, which is the appearance gap that keeps glove-only policies from transferring (robot-validation loss 0.52 against 0.26 co-trained), as in the fixed-camera version of the device. The fixed-camera ablation was mounted low because the positioner took the high vantage point; a higher fixed camera would occlude less, though it cannot follow the hand. Hardware and software are open \citep{yamumi2026,forwardcamumi2026}. \todo{what is next: multi-tile workspaces, in-the-wild sessions, a second task}
