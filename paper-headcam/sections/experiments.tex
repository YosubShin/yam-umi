\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Experimental design.}
To evaluate our system, we compare eight policy configurations (Table~\ref{tab:arms}) spanning three visual viewpoints: the active head camera, a wrist-mounted fisheye, and a static external camera.
In all co-trained conditions, teleoperated demonstrations use robot joint encoders for action labels, while human glove demonstrations are labelled by the head camera (unless specified otherwise for ablation).
During teleoperation, the operator viewed the real-time head-camera preview, and all three cameras recorded the demonstrations concurrently; the policies thus share identical demonstration trajectories and differ strictly in observation, with demonstration style shaped by the head-camera view.
Table~\ref{tab:arms} lists the eight arms.
All glove-based arms train on the same 198 demonstrations.
Arms 1--4 evaluate the scaling benefit of co-training human glove demonstrations across two teleoperation budgets: full (172 episodes) and low (48 episodes).
Arm 6 isolates head-camera labelling accuracy by training on teleoperation episodes labelled via the head camera rather than motor encoders.
Arms 5, 7, and 8 evaluate camera vantage points.
Arm 5 replicates the standard wrist-camera UMI paradigm \citep{chi2024umi} using a wrist fisheye mounted to the glove for this ablation only, whereas the head-view arms use the glove with no electronics.
Arm 7 evaluates a static external camera, training on \SI{10}{\percent} fewer glove frames (43.5k vs.\ 48.5k) due to human body occlusions.
Arm 8 pairs head-camera labels with wrist-camera visual observations, isolating visual perspective from label quality.

\paragraph{Label accuracy.}
We first evaluate the tracking accuracy of the head camera against robot encoder ground truth by mounting the fiducial ball directly onto the YAM arm.
Across 5,065 frames (\SI{91}{\percent} inlier rate), the head-camera pipeline estimates tool-point position with \SI{6.5}{mm} median error (\SI{14.1}{mm} 90th percentile) and orientation within \SI{1.55}{\degree} median (\SI{2.70}{\degree} p90), after the calibrated base transform absorbs static mounting offsets.
Jaw width estimation was similarly verified across 112 teleoperated episodes (24.6k frames).
Visual width tracking achieves a median error of \SI{3.5}{\percent} of stroke (\SI{7.9}{\percent} p90, corresponding to \SI{3}{mm} and \SI{7.5}{mm} of the \SI{95}{mm} stroke), tightening to \SI{2.3}{\percent} median error in the closed state that decides a grasp.
The width labels exhibit zero frame lag against motor commands (artificially shifting by one frame strictly raises the error) and achieve full per-frame coverage in 9 out of 10 episodes (lowest: \SI{68}{\percent}).
Accuracy remains uniform across operating conditions: across bins divided by workspace quadrant, camera distance (\SI{0.4}{}--\SI{0.8}{m}), elevation angle (\SI{40}{}--\SI{87}{\degree}), and jaw aspect angle, median error stays between \SI{2.8}{}--\SI{4.8}{\percent} (\SI{6.8}{}--\SI{8.5}{\percent} p90), rising slightly only when viewing perpendicular to the jaw travel axis.
The remaining residual is a systematic \SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\percent} under-read at maximum aperture, representing a scale error of the per-session fit (evaluated with alternate-frame holdout) rather than measurement noise; by comparison, the fixed workspace camera in \citet{yamumi2026} measured the same gripper with \SI{4.4}{\percent} median error and \SI{94}{\percent} coverage.
Downstream policy training confirms that this perceptual accuracy is sufficient for robust imitation learning: training on head-camera visual labels instead of encoders (Arm 6 vs.\ Arm 1), scored on the same encoder-labelled held-out episodes, incurs only a \SI{3}{\percent} increase in validation loss (0.328 vs.\ 0.318) and a rollout success within the noise of 25 trials (15/25 vs.\ 18/25; 5 placements won to 8 lost on the same scenes, exact McNemar $p = 0.58$).

\paragraph{Co-training and the teleoperation budget.}
Following established precedents in co-training across heterogeneous embodiments—such as mixing static and mobile manipulation data \citep{fu2024mobilealoha} or combining human demonstration video with robot data \citep{kareer2025egomimic}—we train unified policies on merged human glove and robot teleoperation datasets.
Adding the 194 human glove demonstrations to all 172 teleoperated demonstrations reduces validation loss on held-out robot episodes from 0.318 to 0.263, a \SI{17}{\percent} relative improvement (Arm 2 vs.\ Arm 1).
When teleoperation data is scarce, co-training yields an even larger gain: adding glove demonstrations to 48 teleoperated episodes cuts validation loss from 0.464 to 0.295 (Arm 4 vs.\ Arm 3).
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:budget}, log-linear interpolation of the teleoperation-only loss curve demonstrates that the glove dataset provides the equivalent benefit of 65--100 additional teleoperated episodes, achieving an effective exchange rate of 2--3 human demonstrations per teleoperated episode.
At the 48-episode budget, co-training saves 50--120 episodes of robot teleoperation, lifting performance to that of 96--172 teleoperation-only episodes.
Although the marginal reduction diminishes as the base teleoperation budget grows (a 0.075 loss drop at 24 teleoperated episodes down to 0.011 at 96 episodes, measured against the full mix), glove data effectively stretches a small teleoperated set, though it does not replace the last few dozen teleoperated demonstrations.
In physical robot rollouts, the 48-episode co-trained model succeeds in 17/25 trials, outperforming the 48-episode teleoperation baseline (10/25; 9 placements won to 2 lost, $p = 0.065$) and matching the full 172-episode model (18/25; 4 to 5, tied). Every failure of the two 48-episode arms is a missed grasp, and the difference lies in recovery: in Scene 5, the co-trained policy missed its initial grasp, backed away, and successfully re-grasped, whereas the teleoperation-only policy never closed in.

\paragraph{Vantage point comparison.}
Offline validation loss across the four co-trained camera configurations (Arms 2, 5, 7, and 8) falls within a narrow 0.011 band (0.252--0.263), with each gaining 0.04--0.06 over its teleoperation-only counterpart.
Because offline validation loss is known to correlate weakly with physical rollout success \citep{mandlekar2021robomimic}, real-world rollouts reveal the differences: Arm 2 (head camera) succeeds in 20/25 trials, the wrist-camera baseline (Arm 5) in 15/25, the fixed camera (Arm 7) in 12/25, and head labels on the wrist view (Arm 8) in 14/25. On the shared placements the head camera wins 6/1, 11/3 and 8/2 against them ($p = 0.13$, $0.06$ and $0.11$); no single pair clears $0.05$ at 25 placements, but no arm beats Arm 2 on more than three.
Failure modes diverge fundamentally by camera vantage point: head-camera policies almost always fail by never grasping, whereas wrist-camera policies frequently fail by dropping the grasped object mid-carry ($d+b = 12$ of $21$ failures for Arms 5 and 8, 7 of 11 for Arm 8 alone, compared to $1$ of $45$ for the head-camera Arms 1--4 and 6, and 1 of 13 for the fixed camera).
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:rollouts}, this behavior is tied to visual context: in the operator's notes every wrist-view drop occurred while the fisheye was looking away from the receptacle bin during transport, and none while the bin remained visible; the single head-camera drop (Arm 1, Scene 22) occurred immediately after lift-off with both gripper and bin in frame, a slip rather than a release.
Because the head camera tracks the hand from an elevated vantage point, it maintains both the gripper and the target bin within a single coherent field of view throughout the carry.
Controlling for visual observation (Arm 8 vs.\ Arm 5) isolates label quality: using head-camera labels rather than external camera labels yields 14/25 against 15/25 (2 placements to 3), so on the wrist view the two label sources are indistinguishable.
Conversely, controlling for label source (Arm 8 vs.\ Arm 2) isolates viewpoint: replacing head-camera observation with wrist-camera observation drops success from 20/25 to 14/25 and converts the no-grasp failures into mid-carry drops (7 of Arm 8's 11 failures).
Finally, the static camera (Arm 7) achieved the lowest success of the four co-trained views, 12/25, failing like the head camera by not grasping (12 of 13 failures) but more often; because the active positioner occupied the optimal elevated vantage point, the fixed camera was positioned lower, making its \SI{10}{\percent} occlusion rate an upper bound for static views in this workspace.

\paragraph{Qualitative observations.}
In Scene 5, Arm 8 (head labels on the wrist view) released the block mid-transport with the block outside its view, as if it no longer registered the object in hand; Arm 5 shows the same pattern in its three mid-carry drops. Head-camera arms that fail do so before contact, by stopping short of the block or closing beside it, and then hold position until the budget expires; they do not release a block once lifted (one slip in 120 trials). Median time to success is \SI{16}{}--\SI{19}{s} for every arm, so the cameras differ in whether the task completes, not in how fast.
