\begin{abstract}
Handheld demonstration devices decouple data collection from the robot, but conventional designs burden the hand with a wrist camera, cables and a pose tracker (SLAM, an IMU, a VR controller, or a fixed external camera that the collector's body occludes), and burden the pipeline with as many data streams to timestamp, synchronise and cross-calibrate. We show that a single egocentric camera (\$122) worn on the collector's head replaces both the camera and the tracker, and the streams with one. It reads printed fiducials on an electronics-free glove (\$22) against paper tiles taped around the workspace, giving the 6-DoF gripper pose and the jaw width from the same frames the policy will see; at deployment the same camera rides a low-cost (\$120) arm beside the robot that mimics the collector's head from the robot's joint state. Against the robot's encoders the labels are accurate to \SI{6.5}{mm} and \SI{1.6}{\degree} median with \SI{93}{}--\SI{100}{\percent} frame coverage, and a policy trained on them instead of encoders pays \SI{3}{\percent} in validation loss. Adding 194 glove demonstrations to 48 teleoperated episodes lifts rollout success from 10/25 to 17/25, level with 172 teleoperated episodes alone (18/25), and the full co-trained head-camera policy succeeds in 20/25 against 15/25 for the wrist-camera recipe, whose policies drop the object when the goal leaves their view, and 12/25 for a fixed camera.
\end{abstract}
